Activity 2 · Study

The Three Types of Diligence

👤 Individual

Diligence means taking responsibility for what we do with AI and how we do it. It is the fourth “D” of AI Fluency: Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence.

Creation Diligence

Before and during
  • Which AI tool should I use?
  • Is it safe for this data?
  • Is AI right for this task at all?

Transparency Diligence

Being honest
  • Who needs to know AI helped?
  • How do I say it clearly?
  • Am I following the rules?

Deployment Diligence

After: sharing the result
  • Did I check the facts?
  • Could this hurt or be unfair to anyone?
  • Can I stand behind it?
InstructionsWhich type of Diligence is each person practising? Choose, then check.
1. Mei checks that her company allows the AI tool before she uses it for a client project.
Creation: choosing the right tool before starting.
2. Omar adds a note to his report: “Claude helped me organise the data.”
Transparency: being honest about AI’s role.
3. Lucía verifies every statistic in an AI-drafted newsletter before she sends it to 5,000 customers.
Deployment: taking responsibility for what you share.
4. Daniel decides NOT to use AI to write a condolence message to a colleague.
Creation: deciding whether AI is right for the task at all.
5. Aisha tells her teacher which parts of her essay AI helped with.
Transparency.
6. Before publishing an AI-written job ad, Ken checks it for language that could exclude older applicants.
Deployment: checking the output for harm or unfairness before it goes out.
Activity 3 · Study · Handout #1

Data Privacy & AI: A Practical Guide

👤 Read 5 min👤 Practise 7 min
What happens when you paste something into an AI tool?

Your text is sent to the company’s computers (servers). Depending on the tool and your settings, it may be stored, reviewed by people for safety, or used to train future models. Free personal accounts and work accounts that your employer pays for often follow different rules. Once you paste something, you can’t fully control where it goes.

Real example: In 2023, engineers at Samsung pasted confidential source code and meeting notes into ChatGPT to get help. Samsung then banned staff from using public AI chatbots on company devices.

The traffic-light rule
LightExamplesWhat to do
🔴 Red: neverPasswords, ID or passport numbers, bank and card numbers, health records, other people’s personal data or work (without permission), confidential company data (code, client lists, unreleased results), student gradesDon’t paste it. Describe the problem in general words instead.
🟡 Yellow: carefulInternal documents, meeting notes, customer emails, drafts that mention real peopleRemove names and numbers first (anonymise), AND use only a tool your organisation approves.
🟢 Green: OKPublic information, general questions, your own writing without private details, made-up examplesGo ahead, but still check the answer (Discernment!).
Five questions before you paste
How to anonymise: Replace names with roles (“Maria Lopez” → “Customer A”). Remove numbers (account, order, phone). Make details general (“a hospital in Chicago” → “a large hospital”).
Practise: Red, Yellow, or Green?
1. A client’s full name, address, and card number, to “write a refund letter”
Red. Write the letter with “[Customer name]” instead.
2. A government website page about visa rules, to simplify the English
Green. Public information. (Still check the simplified version against the original!)
3. Your company’s unreleased sales figures
Red. Confidential business data.
4. A customer complaint email that still has the customer’s name and order number
Yellow. Remove the name and number first, and use an approved tool.
5. A classmate’s essay, to check it, without asking them
Red. It’s someone else’s work, and you don’t have permission.
6. Team meeting notes that mention colleagues by name
Yellow. Replace names with roles and use a work-approved tool.
7. A made-up example: “A café owner wants to raise prices by 10%. How should she tell customers?”
Green. No real person or data.

Activity 4 · Study

Who Owns It? Should You Say So?

👤 Individual → 💬 Chat
Part A · Intellectual property: who owns AI-generated content?

The answer depends on the country and the tool’s terms of service. In the United States, the Copyright Office says copyright protects human creativity. Material made entirely by AI generally can’t be copyrighted, but your human contributions (your own text, how you select, arrange, and edit the material) can be protected. Other countries have different rules, and the law is still changing.

There is a second risk: AI can produce text or images very similar to someone else’s work. If you publish it, you may be responsible.

1. If AI made it, I automatically own the copyright in every country.
False. Rules depend on the country and the tool’s terms.
2. In the US, my own human edits and arrangement can be protected, even if AI helped.
True. Human authorship is the key idea.
3. If AI copies someone’s work and I publish it, I may be responsible.
True. You are responsible for what you publish.
Part B · Attribution and disclosure: when and how to say AI helped

Disclose when your school or employer requires it, when the work is published or sent to clients, and when your reader would reasonably expect a human did the work. A good disclosure says which tool and what it did.

Real example: In 2023, two New York lawyers submitted a court document with case citations that ChatGPT had invented. They didn’t check the cases or tell the court AI had been used. A judge fined them $5,000.

1. You ask AI for a synonym for one word in a text message to a friend.
Not needed. Low stakes, personal, and tiny help.
2. AI wrote the first draft of a report you send to a paying client.
Disclose. The client expects to know how the work was made (and many contracts now require it).
3. AI suggested the outline for an essay in this class.
Disclose. Academic work: always follow the course’s AI rules.
4. A company blog post was mostly written by AI.
Disclose. Readers may assume a person wrote it. Remember Sports Illustrated (Week 4)!
5. Which disclosure statement is best for an essay?
c) It names the tool, says exactly what it did, and says what is yours.

AI and Jobs: Automate, Augment, or Keep Human?

👤 → 💬 Chat

AI usually changes tasks before it changes whole jobs. Some tasks are automated (AI does them, people spot-check). Many are augmented (AI helps, a person decides). Some should stay human, especially when they need empathy, accountability, or judgment about people’s lives.

Some jobs will shrink and new ones will appear. Professional value moves toward skills AI does not replace: judgment, relationships, ethics, creativity, and the ability to direct and check AI (the 4Ds!).

InstructionsChoose the best option for each task. These are suggested answers: you can disagree if you have a good reason. Share one disagreement in the chat.
1. Sorting 2,000 invoices into categories
Automate, with spot checks. Repetitive and low-risk.
2. Writing the first draft of a job ad that a recruiter edits
Augment. AI drafts, a person checks for accuracy and fair language.
3. Telling an employee they are being laid off
Keep human. It needs empathy, respect, and accountability.
4. Translating a product manual that a native speaker then reviews
Augment.
5. The final decision on someone’s disability benefit claim
Keep human. AI may help organise the file, but a person must decide and be accountable.
6. Scheduling 30 meetings across five time zones
Automate.

Policy Doctor: Fix a Weak AI Policy

👤 Read 4💻 Breakouts 8🎯 Share 3
Handout #4 · Sample organisational AI use policy

Northside Marketing Group · Responsible AI Use Policy (sample)

1. Purpose. We use AI to work faster and better, while protecting our clients, our staff, and our reputation.

2. Who this applies to. All employees, interns, and contractors.

3. Approved tools. Use only the AI tools on the IT-approved list. Use your work account, never a personal account, for company work.

4. Data protection. Follow the traffic-light rule. Never enter passwords, personal data about clients or staff, or confidential company information. Anonymise customer details before using AI.

5. Human review. A person must check every AI output for accuracy, tone, and fairness before it is sent or published. The person who sends it is responsible for it.

6. Disclosure. Tell clients when AI produced a significant part of their deliverable. Label AI-generated images and voices.

7. Fairness. AI must never make final decisions about hiring, firing, pay, or promotion. Any AI tool used in HR must be tested for bias before use and at least once a year.

8. Intellectual property. Do not use AI to copy a competitor’s or artist’s work. Check that AI images and text do not copy protected material.

9. Training. All staff complete AI-fluency training once a year.

10. Reporting. Report AI mistakes, data leaks, or unfair results to ai-help@[company].com within 24 hours. No one will be punished for reporting honestly.

11. Review. This policy is reviewed every six months, because AI changes quickly.

Handout #2 · Case: AI bias in hiring at Amazon
Key words: applicant = a person who applies for a job · penalise = give a lower score as a punishment · disband = stop a team or group

In 2014, a team at Amazon started building an AI tool to review job applicants’ resumes and give them a score from one to five stars. The goal was to find top talent automatically.

The tool learned from resumes submitted to Amazon over the previous ten years. Most of those resumes came from men, which reflected the male-dominated tech industry. By 2015, the company noticed a problem: the system was not rating candidates for technical jobs in a gender-neutral way. It penalised resumes that included the word “women’s”, as in “women’s chess club captain”, and it reportedly downgraded graduates of two all-women’s colleges. It also favoured verbs such as “executed” and “captured”, which appeared more often on men’s resumes.

Engineers edited the program to make it neutral to these particular terms, but they could not be sure it would not find other ways to discriminate. Amazon disbanded the team by early 2017. When Reuters reported the story in 2018, Amazon said its recruiters had never used the tool’s rankings alone to evaluate candidates.

1. Why did the tool prefer men?
b) Nobody told it to prefer men. It learned the pattern from historical data.
2. Removing the word “women’s” did not solve the problem. Why?
c) Proxies. Bias can hide in features that look neutral.
3. Where did the bias mainly come from?
Data: historical inequality in past hiring.
4. Testing the tool, finding the problem, and stopping the project is an example of which Diligence?
Deployment Diligence: checking for harm before relying on the output. (You could also argue Creation: deciding the tool was not right for the job.)

AreaExamples
PolicingPredicting where crime will happen; facial recognition

Citizens usually can’t choose another provider, and decisions affect their freedom, money, and safety. That’s why algorithmic accountability matters: someone must be able to explain, check, and correct the system.

Handout #3 · Case: predictive policing
Key words: predictive = trying to say what will happen · patrol = police moving around an area to watch it · audit = an official check · ban = officially stop

In the 2010s, many U.S. police departments used predictive policing software, such as PredPol (later called Geolitica). The software used past crime reports to predict where crime was likely to happen next, so police could send patrols there.

The problem: crime data does not show all crime. It shows the crime that police recorded. If police already patrol some neighbourhoods more than others, those areas produce more records. The software then predicts more crime there, more police are sent, and even more crime is recorded. This is a feedback loop.

In a 2016 study, researchers Kristian Lum and William Isaac tested this idea with drug-crime data from Oakland, California. Health surveys suggested drug use was spread across the city, but the algorithm would have sent police mostly to Black and low-income neighbourhoods.

After criticism from researchers and communities, some cities changed course. In 2020, Santa Cruz, California, became one of the first U.S. cities to ban predictive policing, and the Los Angeles Police Department stopped using PredPol.

The software predicts more crime in Area A.

Case Clinic: When AI Goes Wrong

💻 Breakouts 15🎯 Report 12
Instructions
  1. Your teacher gives each breakout room ONE case (A, B, C, or D).
  2. In your room (15 min): read your case and answer the 4 questions together. Choose a speaker.
  3. Back in the main room (12 min): each speaker reports in 2–3 minutes. While others speak, take notes on their cases in the table at the end.

Case A · Netherlands: childcare benefits

From around 2013, the Dutch tax authority used a risk-scoring system to choose which childcare-benefit claims to check for fraud. Having a non-Dutch nationality was used as a risk factor. Tens of thousands of parents were wrongly treated as fraudsters and told to pay back large amounts of money. Many families fell into serious debt. In January 2021, the Dutch government resigned over the scandal.

Case B · Australia: “Robodebt”

From 2016 to 2019, the Australian government used an automated system that compared yearly tax data with welfare payments and sent debt notices to people it calculated had been overpaid. Many of the debts were wrong, and people had to prove they did not owe the money. The government later agreed to a class-action settlement worth over A$1 billion. In 2023, a Royal Commission called the scheme “crude and cruel”.

Case C · UK: exam grades by algorithm (2020)

When COVID-19 cancelled exams, England’s exam regulator used an algorithm to adjust teachers’ predicted A-level grades, partly based on each school’s past results. About 40% of grades were lowered, and students at schools in poorer areas were hit hardest. After student protests (“Ditch the algorithm!”), the government reversed the decision and used teachers’ grades.

Case D · USA: a wrongful arrest (Detroit, 2020)

Police in Detroit arrested Robert Williams in front of his family after facial recognition software matched his old driver’s license photo to a blurry video of a shoplifter. It was the wrong man. He was held for about 30 hours before the case was dropped. In a 2024 settlement, Detroit police agreed that a facial recognition match alone cannot be the reason for an arrest.
